Detection Early Enough to Resume:
A SAM3-Based Pipeline for 3D-Print Bed-Adhesion Failure Onset
Sejik Park
Abstract— Existing 3D-print failure monitors detect spaghetti
— the visible tangle of extruded filament that forms after bed
adhesion has already failed and the partial print has been
lost. We re-define the detection target around the earlier event,
the failure onset, and require detection inside the resumability
window: the interval during which an operator could still refix the partial print and resume the job. The mask of a print
object under a bed-synchronised camera is pixel-stationary by
construction during normal operation, so the problem reduces
to detecting the moment the mask becomes unstable. We use
SAM3 with a single bounding-box prompt on frame zero as
a zero-shot mask producer, but generic SAM3 drifts on bedsynchronised footage because its memory cross-attention prior,
trained on natural video, anchors object identity on motion
track — exactly the cue that is inverted in our regime. We
patch the attention layer with a single additive Gaussian spatial
bias whose only free parameter is the width σ, and tune σ and
the bias path (self / cross / both) via hierarchical Sobol search
on a manual 9-anchor-per-sequence mask reference. On a 20-
sequence P1 corpus the self-attention bias at σ = 12.59 recovers
one sequence on which vanilla SAM3 fails. The downstream
1D anomaly detector panel, scored under a lag-aware metric,
detects failure onset roughly an order of magnitude earlier than
the deployed Obico YOLO spaghetti detector and catches the
seven partial-detachment cases that YOLO structurally cannot
fire on. The mean resumability lead time across jointly-detected
sequences is approximately twenty minutes at 1 fps capture. No
fine-tuning is performed: the public SAM3 checkpoint loads
unmodified, and σ → ∞ recovers vanilla bit-exactly.
I. INTRODUCTION
A 3D print that has just turned into spaghetti is no longer
a print. The visible tangle of extruded filament that current
print-failure detectors react to [1], [2] is itself a consequence
of a much earlier event — the failure of bed adhesion —
and until that moment, the print was still physically present
on the bed in a state from which an operator could, in
principle, re-fix the part and resume the job. The detection
problem the field has agreed on is the problem of stopping
the machine to save material and electricity. The problem
this paper addresses is the more ambitious one of detecting
bed-adhesion failure early enough that the operator could
intervene before the partial print is destroyed, which we call
detection inside the resumability window.
a) Why the existing target is structurally late.:
Spaghetti detection treats the visible aftermath of failure as
the failure signal, and any classifier trained on the aftermath
must wait for the aftermath to mature. This is not a tuning
problem that can be solved by lowering the YOLO confidence threshold: the aftermath simply does not exist yet at

b) Why a generic foundation segmentor is not enough.:
We use SAM3 [3], [4] as the mask producer with a single
bounding-box prompt on frame zero. SAM3 was trained on
large-scale natural video in which the foreground moves and
the background is approximately static, so its memory crossattention has implicitly learned to anchor object identity on
motion track. Bed-synchronised footage inverts that statistic:
the foreground (the print) is pixel-stationary throughout the
entire normal phase, and motion comes only from the extruder, the gantry, and the moment of failure itself. We show
in §III-C that this regime shift causes SAM3’s memory crossattention to drift onto extruder shadows and bed corners,
producing masks that are too noisy for downstream stability
analysis.
c) The fix is one parameter.: We patch SAM3’s rotaryposition attention layer with an additive Gaussian spatial
bias that explicitly encodes “the print stays where it was”
(Equation 2). The bias has a single continuous parameter, the
width σ, and one discrete switch: whether the bias is applied
to the self-attention path, the cross-attention path, both, or
neither. The vanilla configuration is recovered exactly. A
hierarchical Sobol search on a 20-sequence manual mask
reference selects the path and σ; on our corpus this single-
parameter intervention recovers a sequence on which vanilla
SAM3 fails to produce usable masks (§IV-C).
d) Empirical pay-off.: On the same 20-sequence corpus, with the same lag-aware scoring rule applied to both,
the proposed pipeline detects failure roughly an order of
magnitude earlier than the Obico YOLO spaghetti detector
[1], and crucially detects the seven partial-detachment cases
that YOLO misses entirely because they never produce
visible spaghetti (§IV-E). The mean resumability lead time
across jointly-detected sequences is approximately twenty
minutes at 1 fps capture.
e) Contributions.: We list the contributions in the order
in which the paper develops them.

• Mechanism for why SAM3 fails on bed-sync footage.

II. RELATED WORK
A. 3D-Print Failure Detection
The deployed state of the art in print-failure detection
is the spaghetti detector, of which the Obico open-source
variant [1] is the most widely used. The Obico detector is
a YOLO [5] model trained on a large internal corpus of
print-failure images; in production it is deployed by hobbyist
and small-shop operators through services such as OctoPrint
plug-ins to halt prints once spaghetti is visible. The original
technical motivation is documented in their open-source
release [6]. A small but growing body of academic work also
addresses 3D-printing failure detection through computer
vision. Banadaki et al. [2] apply image-classification CNNs
to identify failure-state frames; Goh et al. [?] survey deeplearning approaches for additive manufacturing and report
that all surveyed visual methods rely on either spaghetti-style
aftermath classification or geometric comparison against a
slicer reference.
Geometric-reference methods, including STL-to-image
registration [7] and slicer-pose projection [8], compare the
captured image against the expected geometry from the
slicer’s G-code. These methods can in principle detect onset before spaghetti is visible, but require accurate camera
calibration, known camera-to-bed extrinsics, and supportstructure projection, none of which are typically available on a consumer printer. Our work targets the same
onset-detection goal as the geometric-reference methods, but
achieves it without any of their calibration assumptions by
exploiting bed-synchronised camera placement.
Non-visual sensing modalities — acoustic emissions [9],
thermal imaging [10], and accelerometer data [11] — have
been explored for in-situ monitoring. These complement
camera-based monitoring rather than replacing it; the contribution of the present work is purely visual.
B. Foundation Models for Image and Video Segmentation
The Segment Anything Model (SAM) [12] introduced
large-scale prompt-based image segmentation in 2023, eliminating the need for task-specific supervised training in many
segmentation settings. SAM 2 [3] extended the formulation
to video by introducing a memory module that propagates
per-object identity across frames. SAM 3 [4] refines the
memory module further, adopting a small (dkv = 64) compressed memory bank and a rotary-position attention layer
[13] inside the memory-attention block. We use SAM 3 as
the mask producer in this paper; our patch to its memoryattention layer is described in §III-D and is, to our knowledge, the first reported post-hoc spatial-prior injection into
an SAM-family video segmentor for the bed-synchronised
regime.
A complementary line of work uses generic visionlanguage foundation models for industrial inspection [14],
[15]. These methods are less natural for our problem because
they classify or segment from text descriptions; we need to
track a specific instance across an entire multi-hour video
without re-prompting.
C. Attention-Layer Priors and Bias Injection
Adding an additive distance-decaying bias to attention
logits is a well-established technique. ALiBi [16] adds
a linear distance-decay bias to language-model attention
to enable length generalisation; RoPE [13] (and its many
extensions) encodes position multiplicatively via rotations
in the query/key space. Vision transformers have absorbed
both ideas [17], [18]. What our work contributes is not
the form of the bias — a Gaussian on Euclidean spatial
distance is the obvious form for a 2D image grid — but the
argument that this exact form, at this exact injection point
in SAM 3, is the smallest possible intervention that resolves
a specific dataset-shift problem (§III-C). In particular, the
work most adjacent to ours methodologically is research on
injecting domain priors into transformer attention for out-ofdistribution deployment [19], [20]; the work most adjacent to
ours by application is geometric-prior injection for industrial
monitoring [8].
D. Change-Point and Anomaly Detection on 1D Signals
The downstream half of our pipeline relies on classical change-point detectors on a 1D mask-derived signal.
We use a broad detector panel covering five families that
are individually well-understood in the process-control and
online-anomaly-detection literatures: cumulative sum [21],
[22], exponentially weighted moving average [23], median
absolute deviation [24], slope-energy [25], and z-score with
cumulative windowing [26]. Robust online change-point detection is surveyed by Aminikhanghahi and Cook [?] and
Truong et al. [?].
Two design decisions distinguish our use of these detectors
from their canonical industrial applications. First, we do not
commit to a single detector a priori; we run the panel and
select per mask configuration in cross-validation. With 20
sequences this is the only defensible policy. Second, the
scoring rule is lag-aware: every detector is scored by how
soon after tonset it fires, not just whether it fires. This is the
LADS score introduced in §III-G; it is closest in spirit to the
time-to-detection metric used in industrial process control
[27] but adapted to a small fixed corpus rather than a long
online stream.
E. Bed-Synchronised Camera Placement
Bed-synchronised or printer-mounted cameras have been
used in the 3D-printing community primarily for timelapse
capture [28]. To our knowledge no prior work explicitly uses
the pixel-stationarity invariant of bed-synchronised footage
as the core of a failure-detection method. The work most
adjacent to this specific framing is Banadaki et al. [2], which
uses a fixed-position camera but treats the captured frames
as appearance-classification inputs rather than as a stability
test on a zero-shot segmented mask.
III. METHOD
This section derives the pipeline from a single shift in the
detection target: we are not asking “has the print already
turned to spaghetti?”, we are asking “has bed adhesion
just started to fail, while the partial print is still physically
present on the bed?”. Section III-A re-defines the detection
target around print resumability and shows why existing
spaghetti detectors are structurally a poor fit. Section III-C
analyses why a generic foundation-model video segmentor
— concretely, SAM3 — fails on bed-synchronised footage
even though it is highly capable on natural video. Section IIID derives the corrective measure: a single additive Gaussian
bias on the attention logits, with σ as the only continuous
parameter and the bias path placement as the only discrete
choice. Sections III-E–III-H describe the prompt selection,
the two-stage Sobol search, and the YOLO baseline against
which the final pipeline is compared.
A. Problem Formulation: Detection Early Enough to Resume
The dominant deployed approach to 3D-print failure monitoring detects spaghetti — the tangle of extruded filament
that accumulates after the partial print has already lost
adhesion and the nozzle continues depositing into air. Public datasets and commercial detectors (the Obico spaghetti
model, for instance [1]) treat spaghetti as the failure signal.
Doing so equates the failure event with its visible aftermath.
We argue that this conflation is the most consequential
modelling choice in the field, and we re-define the detection
target accordingly. Let tonset be the frame at which bed
adhesion first fails — the print object begins to translate,
tilt, or detach — and let tspaghetti be the (typically later)
frame at which a YOLO classifier could be expected to fire
because tangled filament has become visually unambiguous.
The interval [tonset, tspaghetti] is the window inside which an
operator could physically intervene, re-fix the partial print,
and resume the job. Outside that window the partial print
is destroyed and the only remaining action is to stop the
machine and recover material. We call tonset the resumability
horizon.
This re-definition has two immediate consequences for
method design:
• Spaghetti appearance is not a feature, it is a consequence. A classifier trained to recognise spaghetti is
structurally late: it must wait for the geometry of failure
to mature. Designing for tonset means designing against
a signal that, by definition, looks visually normal until
the very last moment before motion — no spaghetti,
no obvious deformation, the print just sits there until it
moves a few pixels.
• The decision variable is per-frame mask stability, not
per-frame appearance. If the print has not moved, the
mask of the print is identical to the mask one frame
earlier. If the print has moved, the mask differs by
exactly the amount the print moved. A change-point
detector on a mask-derived signal therefore fires on
tonset by construction, provided the masks themselves
are reliable.
The rest of the method is forced by these two observations.
The mask must be produced without per-print supervised
training, because every sequence in the corpus differs in
geometry, colour, and partial-print state (§III-C explains why
a foundation segmentor is the only practical option). The
mask must be stable under bed synchronisation, which is
where generic foundation segmentors fail (§III-C–§III-D).
And the downstream change-point analysis must be robust to
the per-sequence variation in print speed, layer height, and
absolute mask area (§III-G).

B. Pipeline Overview
Figure 2 shows the full pipeline. We summarise the data
flow here; the individual stages are derived in §III-C–§III-G.
a) Capture.: A webcam mounted on the printer chassis
captures images at one frame per second and saves each
frame as a JPEG. The 1 Hz cadence is a deliberate, fixed
choice rather than a downsample of a higher-rate stream:
SAM3 produces materially different masks for a given event
at 1 fps versus 30 fps, and consistent capture across sequences
is required for the Sobol search to converge on a single σ. At
1 fps a typical 4–6 h print produces 14 000–22 000 frames.
b) Bbox prompt.: On the first frame of each sequence
the user draws one axis-aligned bounding box around the
partial print object. The bbox is saved as a small JSON file
and consumed once at the start of the sequence. No further
user interaction is required for the rest of the run. The choice
of bbox over point prompts is justified empirically in §III-E.
c) SAM3 with Gaussian attention bias.: The captured
image sequence is encoded as an MP4 timelapse (SAM3
ingests video, not loose JPEGs) and passed through SAM3
with the bbox prompt fixed on frame zero. We monkey-patch
the rotary-position attention block inside SAM3’s memoryattention layers to accept an additive Gaussian spatial bias
on the attention logits. The bias is parameterised by a single
width σ and a discrete path switch (SELF, CROSS, BOTH,
or NONE). The patch is reversible and does not modify
any pretrained weight; if σ is large or the switch is NONE,
SAM3 recovers its vanilla behaviour exactly. The output is a
packed binary mask per frame, saved as a single .npy per
configuration.
d) Stage A: mask quality.: For each of the four bias
configurations, Sobol search over σ selects the value that
maximises the number of sequences passing a strict perframe IoU threshold against the manual mask GT (§III-F).
The hyperparameter sweep is hierarchical: a coarse broad
phase followed by a narrow phase concentrated around the
broad-phase top-K. The output of Stage A is a single
(path, σ) per configuration and the associated mask .npy
files for all 20 sequences.
e) Stage B: failure-onset detection.: The masks selected
in Stage A feed a small library of per-frame scalar signals
— the frame-to-frame mask delta and the mean mask area
— which are then consumed by a panel of one-dimensional
change-point detectors (§III-G). Each detector’s hyperparameters are tuned by a second Sobol search whose objective
is the lag-aware detection score (LADS, §III-G). The result
is a single detection time per sequence, against which the
same sequence’s YOLO-spaghetti detection time (§III-H) is
compared.
C. Why SAM3 Fails on Bed-Synchronised Footage
A natural objection to the proposed bias is that SAM3 is
already a strong video object segmentor and should not need
help. Empirically, it does (§IV); we explain here why.
a) SAM3’s prior is tuned for natural video.: SAM3’s
memory-attention block builds, for each new frame, a query
over a memory bank of past frames using a rotary-position
attention layer. The implicit prior baked into this layer by
large-scale natural-video pretraining is that the identity of
an object is correlated with its motion track: an object that
moves coherently across frames is more likely to be the same
object than a static patch of background. This prior is the
right one for natural video, where the foreground typically
moves and the background is approximately static.
b) Bed synchronisation inverts that statistic.: The camera in our setup is rigidly mounted to the printer chassis,
so the print object is pixel-stationary throughout the entire
normal phase of the print. The extruder, gantry arms, and
(sometimes) shadows of those parts are the only fast-moving
structures in the frame, and they move across the print object.
In SAM3’s prior these are the most identity-bearing tokens;
in our regime they are pure noise. The print object itself —
the thing we actually want to track — moves only when
adhesion fails, which is precisely the event we are trying to
detect.
c) Consequence.: Without an additional cue, SAM3’s
memory-attention is forced to anchor identity on appearance
match alone, and as the partial print grows (new layers are

foundation-model prior that makes the appearance matching
work in the first place, especially as the partial print is one
of the most appearance-varying targets imaginable (it grows
monotonically through the sequence). (ii) Fine-tuning is not
reversible: the resulting weights would be incompatible with
the public SAM3 checkpoint. (iii) The single information we
are missing — the spatial prior that the object stays where it
was — has a trivially small parameter count (one scalar σ),
and there is no reason to relearn the entire memory-attention
stack to inject it.
The next subsection states the injection precisely and
shows that it is the smallest change that closes the gap.

Which placement is correct is not obvious a priori: each path

g-code prior [8], [16] — would add parameters and would
need either gradient training or its own Sobol budget. The
thesis is that the single-parameter Gaussian is enough; §IV
shows that it is.
E. Prompt Type: Bounding Box, Not Point
SAM3 accepts point, box, and mask prompts. We adopted
the bounding-box prompt only after the point prompt was
empirically eliminated. The point prompt is the more conservative annotation cost — one click instead of one drag —
so it was our first choice.
We ran vanilla SAM3 (no bias) on all 20 sequences with
a single positive point click on the centre of the partial print
in frame zero, scored against the same mask GT used for the
rest of the paper (§III-F). Only 10 of 20 sequences passed the
strict per-frame IoU threshold. Switching to a bbox prompt
on the same 20 sequences, again with vanilla SAM3, raised
the pass count to 13 / 20.
The 3-sequence gap between point and bbox is not noise.
Inspection of the failing point-prompt cases shows that
SAM3 expands the mask incorrectly when the point lands
inside a small connected component of the print and SAM3
is forced to guess the object’s outer extent. The bbox prompt
removes that ambiguity by specifying the extent explicitly.
Once this asymmetry was confirmed we did not invest in
Sobol search on the point prompt — starting from a worse
baseline would amplify the search budget by an unfavourable
constant, and the bbox prompt is no more expensive at
deployment time. The rest of the paper uses bbox prompts
exclusively.
F. Stage A: Mask-Quality Sobol Search
Stage A selects the bias path and width σ that maximise
mask quality against a manual reference. Both the reference
and the scoring rule are described here.
a) Mask GT.: For each sequence we extract nine evenly
spaced anchor frames from the pre-failure segment [0, tonset),
skipping the first interval because frame 0 is the prompt
frame and is trivially correct. The researcher hand-masks
each anchor with a custom labelling tool, storing the binary
mask both as a PNG (for visual review) and as an .npy array
(for IoU scoring). This yields 20 × 9 = 180 hand-labelled
masks for the whole corpus. The nine-anchor sampling is the
smallest design that covers each sequence roughly uniformly
without exploding the annotation budget; preliminary tests
with three or five anchors per sequence missed mid-sequence
drift that nine anchors caught.

coverage of an interval rather than independent draws [29];
the same Sobol scheme is reused for Stage B (§III-G) so the
implementation is shared.

corpus, tie-broken by mean IoU. The output of Stage A is,
for each of the four configurations, a single best σ (the
BASELINE configuration has none) and the corresponding
mask .npy files for all 20 sequences. These mask files are
the only input to Stage B.

slowly varying deltat (the silhouette grows gradually as new
material is deposited) and an areat that increases nearly
monotonically. At adhesion failure, the silhouette shifts
abruptly: deltat spikes, and areat either steps up (when the
print falls into a new position that the mask now covers) or
drops (when the print leaves the camera entirely).
b) Detector library.: We deliberately do not commit to
a single change-point detector. The failure-mode literature
reports very different residual statistics for different physical
failure modes [21]–[24], [30]; with 20 sequences we cannot
afford to bet the entire pipeline on one detector a priori. We
run a panel of 22 one-dimensional detectors covering five
families:
• Z-score family: ZOR-AreaJump-Cum,
Z-score-Cum, AreaJump-Cum and their windowed
*-CumWin variants.
• MAD family: MAD, MAD-Cum, MAD-CumWin
(median-absolute-deviation robust statistics).
• CUSUM family: CUSUM, CUSUM-Cum,
CUSUM-CumWin.
• EWMA family: EWMA, EWMA-Cum, EWMA-CumWin.
• Other: Slope-Energy, Phase-Z-score,
Hybrid-Z-Area, Adaptive-Area,
Flicker-Trigger, Page-Hinkley,
IsolationForest, Quantile.
Each detector is a small online algorithm with one to four
hyperparameters (thresholds, window sizes, decay factors).
c) Lag-aware detection score (LADS).: The detector
output is a binary alarm time series. Per-sequence aggregation follows the conventions of §III-A: any alarm at t < tonset
counts as a false positive (FP) and is penalised additively. The
earliest alarm at t ≥ tonset is the single true positive (TP) and
contributes a lag-dependent reward; later post-GT alarms add
no further credit. A missed sequence (no post-GT alarm at
all) incurs a heavy fixed penalty.
Concretely, with lag = tTP − tonset and a horizon constant
W (we use W = 6000 frames, the largest plausible resumability window at 1 fps),
TPscore =
2
1+exp(5·lag/W)
, (6)
LADS = TPscore − 0.1 · FPcount − 99 · ⊮miss. (7)
A perfect immediate detection scores LADS = 1; a detection
at the horizon W scores LADS ≈ 0.013; missing the
sequence dominates the loss landscape. The score rewards
early detection inside the resumability window (§III-A) and
punishes pre-GT chatter that would force the operator to
ignore the system.
d) Per-detector Sobol search.: For each (mask configuration, detector) pair, a hierarchical Sobol search of the same
shape as Stage A optimises the detector hyperparameters
under five-fold leave-out cross-validation: four folds train,
one fold evaluates, the per-fold LADS is summed across
folds, and the hyperparameter set with the highest summed
LADS is selected. Folds are random with a fixed seed (42)
to keep results across configurations directly comparable.
e) Detector selection and reporting.: The reported
number for a given mask configuration is the LADS of
its best per-fold detector at the heldout fold’s evaluation;
the best detector is allowed to differ between configurations. Both the best-detector LADS and a detector ×
configuration table are reported in §IV-D.
H. YOLO Baseline
The most natural baseline for our pipeline is a deployed
YOLO-based spaghetti detector. The objection that “YOLO
finds spaghetti, you detect onset, the comparison is unfair” is
exactly the point: the paper’s claim is that the resumabilityaware target is feasible at all, and that quantifying the
difference in detection time against the deployed alternative
is the central experiment.
We reuse the publicly available Obico spaghetti-detection
model [1] without any retraining or threshold sweep on our
data. The model is a single-class YOLO detector trained on
a large internal Obico corpus; we load the published ONNX
weights and run inference per frame with the project’s default
confidence threshold 0.4 and non-maximum-suppression IoU
threshold 0.3, matching the configuration shipped with the
upstream service.
The per-frame YOLO output is a list of (bounding box,
confidence) tuples. We treat the presence of any detection in
a frame as a spaghetti alarm at that frame. The same scoring
rule used for Stage B applies: pre-GT alarms count as FPs,
the earliest post-GT alarm is the TP, missing the sequence
is a heavy penalty. This gives the YOLO baseline a LADS
score directly comparable with the SAM3 pipeline.
Two implementation notes. First, we do not retune
YOLO’s threshold to our corpus; the point is to compare
against the deployed configuration, not against the strongest
YOLO we could engineer. Second, YOLO runs directly on
the original JPEG frames at the captured resolution, while
SAM3 runs on the timelapse MP4; this asymmetry is intrinsic
to the two pipelines and is not a normalisation we attempt
to correct.
IV. EXPERIMENTS
We evaluate the proposed pipeline on a 20-sequence
corpus of 3D-print failures captured on a Creality Ender
3 Pro with a fixed-position webcam. The evaluation is in
two stages, mirroring the method (§III-F–§III-G). Stage A
measures the quality of the produced masks against a manual
reference and selects the best bias path / σ per configuration.
Stage B feeds the selected masks into the change-point
detector panel and produces a single detection time per
sequence, which is compared against the deployed YOLO
spaghetti-detection baseline (§III-H).
A. Experimental Setup
a) Hardware.: All 20 sequences are captured on a
single Creality Ender 3 Pro printer (designation P1) using a
Logitech C170 webcam mounted on the printer chassis. The
capture script requests 1920×1080, but the camera negotiates
down and the actual saved resolution is 1024×768. Capture
rate is one frame per second; see §III-B for why this rate
is held fixed. Lighting is the workshop’s daytime overhead
lighting throughout.
b) Compute.: SAM3 inference runs on a single
NVIDIA RTX 3090 under PyTorch 2.1 with FP16 mixed
precision. A typical 5 h print (≈18 000 frames at 1 fps)
takes 12–15 min to process at the SAM3 internal resolution
imgsz = 644; the Gaussian-bias configurations are within
5 % of vanilla SAM3’s wall-clock time because the bias
matrix is precomputed once per resolution and cached.
Stage B detectors run on CPU and complete in seconds per
sequence.
c) Software.: SAM3 is loaded via the Ultralytics
wrapper at commit pinned in our environment. The
Gaussian-bias injection is a runtime monkey-patch on the
RoPEAttention.forward method described in §IIID; no Ultralytics source file is modified. YOLO inference
uses Obico’s published ONNX weights [1] through their
detection model module.
d) Reproducibility.: Sobol sequences are generated
with the scipy qmc.Sobol backend with a fixed seed (42).
All hyperparameter searches use the same five-fold split. The
20 sequence names, GT failure frames, and per-anchor mask
paths are recorded in the project repository.
B. Dataset
The corpus is the 20-sequence subset of our internal 3Dprint failure collection that satisfies two filters: P1 printer
(Creality Ender 3 Pro) and still (workshop daytime) background. Sequences with a dynamic background or whose
failure type is “no support” are excluded; they will be
reported in the companion dataset paper.

F. Qualitative Results and Failure Analysis
We illustrate three categories of behaviour observed across
the corpus: the mode where every bias configuration is
roughly equally correct, the mode where SELF-only converts
a vanilla failure into a pass, and the mode where every
configuration fails.
a) Cooperative mode.: On 12 of the 20 sequences the
mask GT is matched by all four configurations (≥ 0.7 IoU
on all nine anchors, with mean IoU ≥ 0.95). These are the
visually-easy sequences — compact prints with a stable
silhouette, high colour contrast against the bed, and short
failure trajectories. BlueHourse+, WhiteBoat+,
WhiteDavid, GreenNeedle, OrangeLibery,
RedHeart+, and BlackMoai are representative. On
these sequences vanilla SAM3 is already strong; the

these for completeness; they are not where any method’s
contribution lies.

0.7; the SELF mask remains anchored at the print’s original

the 10 → 13 pass-count jump documented in §III-E.
V. CONCLUSION
We re-defined the 3D-print failure detection problem
around print resumability instead of spaghetti aftermath,
and derived a pipeline whose every component is forced
by that re-definition. The core technical contribution is the
observation that SAM3’s memory-cross-attention prior is
inverted in bed-synchronised footage — the natural-video
correlation between motion and identity becomes a noise
source — and that the smallest possible corrective is a
single additive Gaussian bias on the attention logits with
one continuous parameter (σ) and one discrete switch (path
placement). The vanilla SAM3 checkpoint loads unmodified,
and the configuration in which the bias is on the selfattention path with σ = 12.59 recovers a sequence on which
vanilla fails to produce usable masks. Compared against the
deployed Obico YOLO spaghetti detector under an identical
lag-aware scoring rule, the proposed pipeline detects roughly
an order of magnitude earlier and catches the seven partialdetachment cases that YOLO structurally cannot fire on.
a) Limitations.: The corpus is 20 sequences captured
on one printer (Creality Ender 3 Pro) with one camera (Logitech C170) under one lighting regime (workshop daytime);
cross-printer generalisation to a second printer is reserved
for the companion dataset paper. The mask GT is singleannotator, so inter-annotator IoU cannot be reported. The
bias is parameterised by a single σ; a per-head or per-layer σ
might improve mask quality further but would require either
gradient training or a much larger Sobol budget than we can
afford. Two sequences (WhiteNeitiri SupportFail
and BlackWobbly+) fail catastrophically on every configuration, both for sequence-specific reasons rather than
a deficiency of the proposed method (the first because of
annotation policy on partial-detachment cases, the second
because the printer hardware itself jitters during normal
printing). Confidence-calibrated detections — per-detection
confidence scores that would let operators tune sensitivity —
are not implemented and remain future work.
b) Future work.: The most immediate next step is the
cross-printer generalisation check: applying the same P1-
tuned hyperparameters without re-tuning to a smaller P2
corpus captured on a different printer-camera combination.
The companion dataset paper documents the full 50-sequence
superset (including the 16 dynamic-background and 5 nosupport sequences excluded here), establishes the corpus as a
community benchmark, and reports the proposed pipeline as
a baseline. A more architecturally ambitious follow-up would
replace the hand-tuned single-σ bias with a small learned
spatial prior on top of the frozen SAM3 backbone; with the
present 20-sequence benchmark in hand, the budget for a
careful learning study is now tractable.
c) Closing remark.: Spaghetti is what the print becomes when no-one was looking. Resumability is what we
lose by waiting for it. The empirical message of this paper
is that the resumability window is not just a conceptual
reframing — it is, on every sequence we have measured, at
least several minutes wide, and a single-parameter attention
bias on a frozen foundation segmentor is enough to detect
inside it.
